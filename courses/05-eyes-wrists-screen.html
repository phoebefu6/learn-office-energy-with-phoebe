Eyes, wrists and the screen

The parts of you that work hardest at a desk are not your back. Your eyes hold one focus distance for hours and quietly stop blinking, and your hands repeat the same small movements thousands of times. Tonight we read what eye doctors actually say about screens, look at a small 2023 study that tested the famous 20-20-20 rule and kept watching after the reminders stopped, learn the NHS signs that turn a tired wrist into a GP appointment, and then audit your own screen in twenty minutes.

Part 1 · covers the AAO page on blinking and distance

Screens change how you blink

5 min live

The American Academy of Ophthalmology, the professional body for eye doctors in the United States, keeps a plain-language page on computers and eye strain, last published in June 2024. Its central point is not about light or radiation. It is about a reflex: when you look at a screen, you blink much less often, and the surface of the eye dries out between blinks.

LiveWhat the AAO says, in four lines3 min▶

You blink less. People normally blink about 15 times a minute; while looking at a screen that drops to about 5 to 7. Fewer blinks means a drier, more irritated eye surface by the end of the day, which is exactly Priyanka's 16:00 feeling.

Distance matters. The page suggests sitting about 25 inches from the screen, roughly arm's length. Closer than that and the eyes work harder to hold focus.

Look up and away. The advice is to take regular breaks by looking at something farther off. Notice what the page does not say: it does not name the 20-20-20 rule. Part 2 looks at where that rule comes from and what testing it found.

No permanent damage. The AAO is clear that screens do not permanently damage your eyes. The strain is real and uncomfortable; it is not an injury that accumulates.

Self-studyWhat eye strain is, and what it is not3 min▶

"Digital eye strain" is a name for a cluster of symptoms: dry or gritty eyes, tired eyes, blurring that comes and goes during the day. On the AAO's account it is mostly about fewer blinks and a fixed focus distance, which is why the fixes are about blinking and looking away rather than about buying something.

What it is not: a reason to ignore changes that do not go away. If your vision is different from how it used to be, if blur stays after a rest, or if you struggle to read at your usual distance, that is a job for an optometrist, who can test your eyes properly. Desk habits can make a screen day more comfortable; they cannot correct an eyesight change, and nothing on this page can tell you whether you have one.

Real world

The deadline stare. A familiar pattern in any office: the closer the deadline, the closer people lean to the screen and the longer they go without looking away. Colleagues often notice it before the person does, the slight squint and the forward head. The AAO's account explains why the end of a crunch day feels worse for the eyes than a normal one: the concentration that gets the work done is the same concentration that suppresses the blink.

Part 2 · covers 20-20-20 and Talens-Estarelles et al. 2023

What a test of 20-20-20 found

5 min live

The rule is everywhere: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. It is usually credited to optometrists, but no single original source has been found (reported). In 2023 Cristian Talens-Estarelles and colleagues did something most advice never does: they gave a group of people reminders to follow it, measured their eyes, then stopped the reminders and measured again.

LiveThe rule, and the finding3 min▶

The rule. Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 metres) away for at least 20 seconds. It is easy to remember, which is most of its appeal. Its origin is usually credited to optometrists; no single original source has been found (reported).

The finding. Talens-Estarelles and colleagues followed 29 computer users who already had symptoms. For two weeks they got reminders to follow 20-20-20. Their digital eye strain and dry eye symptoms fell. One week after the reminders stopped, the improvement was not maintained. Measures of the tear film and the eye's surface did not change at any point, and binocular vision, how the two eyes work together, was mostly unchanged.

The fair one-line verdict: in this small group, following the rule was linked to feeling better while they followed it, without a measurable change in the eye itself, and the benefit did not outlast the habit. There was no separate control group, so some of the improvement may simply be the effect of paying attention to your eyes.

Self-studyWhy the reminders mattered more than the rule3 min▶

Read the timeline again and notice what actually changed between the middle column and the last one. Not the people, not their screens, not their jobs. Only the reminders. Whatever 20-20-20 does, it seems to do it only while somebody is prompting you to do it, which makes this as much a study about habits as about eyes.

That fits what session 4 found on the bench: a break you intend to take is not the same as a break you take, and cues that land in the wrong place silently vanish. It also fits the Cochrane review from session 4, where computer prompts were one of the few workplace tools with any evidence of changing sitting. Session 6 is about building cues that keep firing after the novelty wears off.

One honest caution: the AAO page does not name 20-20-20 at all. It says look up and away. The specific numbers are a convenient convention, not a tested dose. If your window is 15 feet away, looking out of it is still the point.

Real world

The reminder app nobody opens in month two. A familiar office pattern: a team installs a break-reminder app with enthusiasm, everyone obeys it for a fortnight, and then it becomes a notification people dismiss without looking up. The 2023 study is a neat small-scale version of the same arc, with one extra lesson: when the prompting stopped, so did the benefit people reported.

Part 3 · covers the NHS carpal tunnel page

Wrists: a stretch or a doctor?

5 min live

Priyanka's evening tingle is the one symptom tonight that is not about comfort. Inside the wrist there is a narrow passage, the carpal tunnel, and a nerve runs through it to the hand. When that nerve is squeezed, the result can be numbness, tingling and weakness. The NHS has a clear page on what to look out for, and the most useful thing in it is the line that says when to stop managing it yourself.

LiveNeutral wrist, keyboard close3 min▶

For comfort, most desk-setup guides suggest the same few things: keep the keyboard and mouse close enough that your elbows stay near your sides, keep the wrist roughly in line with the forearm rather than bent sharply up or sideways, and vary your hand position through the day. Session 2's point applies here too: no single position is the right one, and staying in any position for hours is the part to avoid.

Be clear about what this is. These are comfort habits. No source read for these pages shows that a particular wrist position prevents carpal tunnel syndrome, and nobody should promise you that it does. If your hands are comfortable, these habits help keep them that way; if they are not, the NHS list in the figure is what decides your next step, not a new keyboard.

Self-studyWhat the NHS says about splints2 min▶

The NHS page describes wrist splints as one of the things a clinician may suggest: worn at night, and it may take up to 6 weeks for them to help. That detail matters for a different reason than it seems. It tells you this is a condition with a proper assessment and a treatment path, not one to self-manage with a splint bought online and a stretch from a video.

A clinician decides whether a splint, or anything else, is right for you. Your job at the desk is simpler: notice the pattern, know the list, and go when the symptoms are getting worse or not going away.

Real world

"It is probably just the mouse." A familiar office pattern: someone mentions numb fingers at lunch, a colleague recommends a vertical mouse, and the conversation ends there. A new mouse may well be more comfortable. But numbness and tingling, especially when they wake you at night, are on the NHS list for a reason, and a gadget is not an assessment. The better lunch advice is the boring one: if it is getting worse or not going away, book the GP.

Build-along

The screen audit

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Six steps at your own desk, in the order you would fix things: where the screen sits, how often you actually look away, two short moves, and two lines you write for yourself about when to stop and see someone. Priyanka's notes are constructed; your numbers will differ. Everything goes on paper: nothing on these pages records what you write.

Measure your distance to the screen. Sit as you normally work and stretch one arm straight out. Your fingertips should land near the screen. The AAO suggests about 25 inches, roughly arm's length; OSHA's workstation guide gives a range of 20 to 40 inches (50 to 100 cm). Write down where you actually are, not where you think you are.

★ Step 1 · distance (Priyanka, constructed)Arm out: fingertips stop about a hand-width short of the screen. Distance: roughly 50 cm, the near end of 50-100 cm. Note: laptop on the desk, pulled close to read.

Check the height of the top of the screen. OSHA's guide puts the top of the monitor at or slightly below eye level, with the centre of the screen 15 to 20 degrees below your horizontal eye line. In practice: look straight ahead and see where your gaze lands. A laptop flat on a desk usually sits well below this.

★ Step 2 · heightLooking straight ahead, my eyes land: [ ] on the top edge of the screen, or just above it [ ] above the screen entirely (screen too low) [ ] in the middle of the screen (screen too high) Fix to try: ________________________________

Run the reminder test for one afternoon. Set a 20-minute repeating timer from lunch to the end of the day. Each time it goes off, look far for 20 seconds if you can, and put one mark on paper either way. The point is not to pass; it is to see how many you actually take, which is what the 2023 study suggests matters.

★ Step 3 · reminder tally (on paper, nothing stored)13:00 to 17:30 = 13 reminders Taken: | | | | | | | (7) Skipped: | | | | | | (6) Skipped during: client call, deadline hour Priyanka's numbers are constructed; yours will differ.

Do the look-far move now. Find something at least 6 metres away: a window, the far end of the room, a door down the corridor. Hold your focus on it for 20 seconds, then blink fully and slowly five times, closing the lids all the way. Move only as far as feels easy, and stop if you feel eye pain or dizziness.

★ Step 4 · look farTarget 6 m or more away: ____________________ Hold: 20 seconds Then: 5 full, slow blinks Range: eyes relaxed, no squinting or straining Stop if: eye pain or dizziness

Do the wrist move. Rest your forearm or hold it loosely in front of you. Make small, slow circles with the hand, 8 one way and 8 the other. Then spread your fingers wide and relax them, 8 times. Keep the circle small and comfortable; if you feel tingling or numbness, stop, and treat it as information for step 6.

★ Step 5 · wrist circles and finger spreadCircles: 8 clockwise, 8 anticlockwise, small and slow Spread: fingers wide, then relax, 8 times Range: only as far as feels easy, no forcing Stop if: tingling or numbness

Write your two red-flag lines. In your own words, write the two rules that tell you a desk habit is no longer the right tool. Keep them somewhere you will see them, such as the inside cover of a notebook.

★ Step 6 · my red-flag lines1. If my vision changes, or blur stays after a rest: book an optometrist. 2. If tingling or numbness in my hand persists or gets worse: book a GP.

Fix the cheap thing first. For most laptop users the biggest single change is height: a stack of books under the laptop plus a separate keyboard moves the screen up and the hands back toward the body. It costs nothing, and it uses both the AAO and OSHA numbers at once. Your audit stays on your paper: nothing on these pages records it.

Homework

Before session 6

20 min
  1. Repeat the reminder test on a different day. Same 20-minute timer, one afternoon. Compare taken against skipped with your first tally, and write down which meetings or tasks swallowed the skipped ones.
  2. Make one screen change and keep it for three days. Raise the screen, push it back to arm's length, or add a separate keyboard. Notice how your eyes and neck feel by late afternoon. Do not expect a dramatic result; note what you notice.
  3. Find your 6-metre target at every place you work. Your desk, a meeting room, a cafe or home. A window works best. Knowing it in advance removes one excuse.
  4. Put your two red-flag lines somewhere visible. A sticky note on the monitor, the notes app on your phone. If either line already applies to you, book the appointment this week instead of waiting for session 6.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · According to the American Academy of Ophthalmology, what happens to blinking when you look at a screen?

The AAO page gives about 15 blinks a minute normally and 5 to 7 at a screen. Fewer blinks lets the eye's surface dry out, which is most of what people call eye strain. The same page says screens do not permanently damage the eyes.

2 · In Talens-Estarelles and colleagues' 2023 study of 20-20-20 reminders, what happened to symptoms one week after the reminders stopped?

Symptoms fell during the two weeks of reminders and the improvement was not maintained a week after they stopped. Tear film and eye-surface signs never changed, and with no separate control group, part of the improvement may come from simply paying attention.

3 · Using the NHS carpal tunnel page, which pattern means it is time to see a GP rather than keep stretching?

The NHS lists ache or pain, numbness, tingling or pins and needles, and a weak thumb or difficulty gripping, usually worse at night, and says to see a GP if symptoms are getting worse or not going away. Desk moves are for comfort, not treatment.
