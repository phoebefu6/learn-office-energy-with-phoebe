Part 1 · covers the model

Slots, cues and the meeting that eats them

5 min live

The bench is simple on purpose, so you can check it by hand. Every working minute is sitting, standing or moving. Desk time is sitting. A meeting is seated, standing, or walkable. A break is a cue at a clock time, every hour or every half hour, and it only fires if that minute is desk time. If the cue lands inside a meeting or lunch, it is dropped, the way a reminder gets dismissed when you are on a call.

LiveThe rules, so you can check any number by hand3 min▶

A sitting bout is a run of consecutive sitting minutes; one minute standing or moving ends it. Lunch "walk" means ten minutes walking out to buy food and the rest seated eating, which is Priyanka's habit. Rung 2 stands for two minutes at the top of every hour. Rungs 3 and 4 do a three-minute routine at every hour and half hour. A break that starts at desk time but runs into a meeting is cut short; the widget counts those too.

The model leaves things out on purpose: bathroom trips, coffee runs, the walk to a meeting room. Real days have a few more natural breaks than the bench shows. That makes every rung look slightly worse than reality, by the same amount, so the comparison between rungs still holds.

Self-studyWhy a model and not a step counter2 min▶

A wrist tracker records what happened; it cannot tell you what would have happened on a different schedule. The bench answers "what if" questions on a fixed day, which is exactly what you need to choose a schedule. It is labelled modelled on every number for that reason. If you want to check it against reality, keep the paper log from session 1 for a day on your chosen rung and compare your longest bout with the bench's.

Build-along · the bench

Priyanka's day, rung by rung

Part 2 · covers the ladder

290, 178, 87, 87

5 min live

Climbing the ladder, the longest bout falls from 290 minutes to 178 to 87, then stays at 87. Bouts over 30 minutes stay at 3 for three rungs and only drop to 2 on rung 4. Both halves of that are the lesson.

LiveWhy standing every hour helps less than it sounds3 min▶

Rung 2 drops the longest bout from 290 to 178 minutes, yet 3 of its 9 hourly cues are dropped, because Priyanka's meetings start on the hour, and so do most meetings. Calendars are built on the hour, which is exactly when an hourly reminder fires. The cues that survive are the ones that land in empty desk time, which is where she was already freest to move.

Rung 3 doubles the number of cues and makes each one a three-minute routine. Even with 7 of 17 dropped, the longest bout falls to 87 minutes and moving minutes rise from 10 to 40. The remaining 87-minute bout is the project review: a reminder cannot open a meeting it is not allowed into.

Self-studyWhat the workplace trials found, and what they never tested3 min▶

A 2018 Cochrane review of workplace interventions (Shrestha and colleagues, 34 studies) found one study where short breaks of one to two minutes every half hour cut sitting at work by about 40 minutes a day compared with two 15-minute breaks, low-quality evidence. Computer prompts, in another single study, cut the number of sitting bouts of 30 minutes or more by about one a day. The direction matches the bench; the evidence is thin.

The review also found no studies at all of standing or walking meetings. Rung 4 is therefore a reasonable idea the bench can model, not a tested intervention. Treat it as an experiment to run on yourself, not a result.

Real world

The fitness tracker that buzzes into a call. Many wrist trackers nudge you to move after a stretch of inactivity. Anyone who has worn one through a video call knows what happens: the buzz arrives, you glance at it, you stay in the meeting. The bench's dropped cue is that moment, counted. The fix is not a louder buzz, it is moving the break to where it can happen, which is session 6.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

The lunchtime gym and the meeting wall

5 min live

Two buttons, two lessons. The gym session is genuinely good for Priyanka, and it barely moves the number this bench watches. The back-to-back afternoon is common, and it undoes most of what the routine built.

LiveThe gym is the right answer to a different question3 min▶

Press the gym button on rung 1. Moving minutes triple, from 10 to 30. The longest sit falls only from 290 to 270, and bouts over 30 minutes stay at 3, because the afternoon after the gym is one unbroken stretch from 13:00 to 17:30. Session 1's two studies explain why both facts matter: Ekelund's says total movement changes the long-term risk, Dunstan's says the pattern of sitting changes what happens within the day. The gym serves the first and does little for the second.

So keep the gym if you have one. Do not let it stand in for the breaks. On rung 3 the gym leaves the longest bout at 87 and adds 20 moving minutes on top: the two levers stack.

LiveThe meeting wall3 min▶

Now press the break button on rung 3. Four seated one-hour meetings fill 13:00 to 17:00. Every afternoon cue lands inside one and is dropped: 12 of 17 cues are lost, the longest sit jumps from 87 to 260 minutes, and moving minutes fall from 40 to 25. Rung 4 cannot help either, because the walkable one-to-one has been replaced. Nothing failed loudly; the reminders simply had nowhere to land.

This is the most useful number on the page for most office workers, because back-to-back afternoons are normal. The fix is a calendar change, not more willpower, and session 6 builds it.

Self-studyRun your own day and read three things4 min▶

Switch to "My own day", type your start, end and lunch, and one line per meeting such as 14:00-15:00 seated. Then read three things. First, your rung 1 longest bout: compare it with the morning log you wrote in session 1. Second, how many cues your meetings drop on rung 3: that is the size of the calendar problem. Third, which of your meetings could honestly be walkable: mark them and try rung 4. Write the three numbers down for session 6.

Homework

Before session 5

20 min
  1. Run tomorrow's real calendar. Enter it in the bench and write down rung 1's longest bout, rung 3's longest bout and rung 3's dropped cues.
  2. Find your meeting wall. Which block of your week has the most back-to-back seated meetings? Note the day and the hours.
  3. Do the routine twice tomorrow, once before lunch and once mid-afternoon, with the timer. Note anything that felt uncomfortable; session 3's stop-if lines apply.
  4. Mark one walkable meeting. A one-to-one, a status check, a phone call. Ask the other person if they would walk it next time.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On rung 1, adding a 30-minute gym session at lunch changes Priyanka's day how?

The gym adds movement, which matters for long-term risk, but the afternoon after it is still one unbroken sit. Breaking up sitting and adding exercise are separate levers; they stack.

2 · Why does rung 2, "stand every hour", drop 3 of its 9 cues on Priyanka's day?

Calendars are built on the hour, and so is an hourly reminder. A cue only fires in desk time; the bench has no random element, so you can check every dropped cue against the meeting list.

3 · What does the 2018 Cochrane review say about walking meetings?

The 100 minutes is the short-term figure for sit-stand desks, from low-quality evidence. For standing or walking meetings the review found nothing to pool, which is a reason to try them as an experiment, not to cite them as proven.
