Learn Office Energy with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

The three-minute routine

Six moves, 30 seconds each, done beside your chair in whatever you wore to work. Two seated, two that get you standing, two for the wrists and the eyes. Tonight every move comes with a drawn guide, a count, how far to go and when to stop. Then we look honestly at what a break this short has been shown to do, which is less than the wellness posters claim and more than nothing, and you build a version that fits your own body and desk.

Part 1 · covers neck and shoulders

Neck and shoulders, seated

5 min live

The routine starts in the chair on purpose. The first minute asks nothing of anyone around you: you turn your head and roll your shoulders, the two places most desk workers notice stiffness first. Both moves are small and slow. The aim is to move joints that have been held still, not to stretch anything hard.

LiveHow to do them, and how far to go3 min▶

Move 1, neck turns (30 seconds). Sit tall without forcing it. Turn your head slowly to look over one shoulder, come back to the middle, then look over the other shoulder. Five each side. Your chin stays level; you are turning, not tipping. Go only as far as feels easy, never into pain. If you can see the wall beside you, that is plenty.

Move 2, shoulder rolls (30 seconds). Lift both shoulders up towards your ears, roll them back, then let them drop down and loose. Eight slow rolls. Small circles are fine; a big circle is not better. If one side feels tighter, keep the circle at the size the tighter side is comfortable with.

The rule for both: slow and smooth, no bouncing, no jerking to get a click. Thirty seconds is long enough to do each one properly and short enough that you will not get bored.

Self-studyStop-if lines, and when neck symptoms need a GP3 min▶

Stop neck turns if you feel dizzy, get pain running down an arm, or get pins and needles. Stop shoulder rolls if a shoulder catches or pinches. Stopping means stopping that move for today, not pushing through to finish the count. Swap it for a smaller version or drop it; the build-along shows how.

The NHS neck pain page says to see a GP if neck pain has not gone after a few weeks, if painkillers are not working, or if you have pins and needles or a cold arm. Those are the lines to know. A routine is not a treatment, and it is not a reason to wait if one of those applies to you.

The same NHS page lists desk posture among common causes of neck pain and advises not keeping your neck in one position for a long time. Session 2 covered where physiotherapists disagree with the "sit upright" part. Both sides agree on the part this move works on: staying still for long stretches.

Real world

The four o'clock neck crack. A familiar office pattern: after a long stretch at the screen someone tips their head hard to one side and waits for the click. The click is not the goal. Slow turns through a comfortable range move the same joints without the jerk, and you can do them in a meeting with your camera on.

Part 2 · covers back and hips

Stand up for the middle two

5 min live

At the one-minute mark the routine makes you stand. That is the most important design choice in the whole three minutes. The reach and the hip opener are pleasant, but the stand-up is what ends the sitting bout, and the sitting bout is the number session 1 had you log and session 4 measures for a whole day.

LiveWhy the routine makes you stand3 min▶

Move 3, stand and reach (30 seconds). Stand up. Reach both arms up, then lean gently to one side and the other, four each side. Lean only a little: you want a mild stretch down the side of your body, not a deep bend.

Here is why standing is in the middle. In session 1 we saw Dunstan and colleagues' 2012 lab trial, where 2-minute walks every 20 minutes lowered the glucose and insulin response after a drink compared with unbroken sitting. Those breaks were walking, not stretching, in 19 overweight adults over one five-hour session. So keep the claim modest: nobody has shown that this exact routine does what those walks did. What the routine does do, for certain, is end the sitting bout, because you cannot do moves 3 to 6 in a chair. Session 4's bench counts exactly that.

Self-studyThe hip opener, and its safe range3 min▶

Move 4, hip opener (20 seconds each side). Hold the desk or chair back if you like. Step one foot back, keep both feet pointing forward, and tuck your hips under, as if bringing your tailbone down and forward, until you feel a stretch at the front of the back hip. Hold, then switch sides.

The safe range is set by that feeling: stop at the first clear stretch in the front of the hip, no further. You do not need a deep lunge; a short step and the hip tuck do the work. Stop if your knee or lower back hurts.

Why this one is here: the front of the hip is the part that sits folded all day. Whether stretching it changes anything long term is not something the evidence read for these pages can answer, so treat it as a move that feels good to many people, not as a fix.

Real world

"I'll stretch at the gym tonight." A common office pattern: the plan to make up for the day in one evening session. Session 1 showed why a lot of movement matters, so the gym is worth keeping. But an evening workout does not break the long afternoon sit, and session 4's bench shows a 30-minute lunchtime gym trip barely touches Priyanka's longest bout. The routine is not instead of the gym; it is for the hours the gym cannot reach.

Part 3 · covers wrists, marching and Albulescu et al. 2022

What a three-minute break can and cannot do

5 min live

The last minute moves your hands and your eyes, then puts some life back in your legs. And since this is where most people ask "does any of this actually work?", it is the right place for the evidence. In 2022 Albulescu and colleagues pooled studies of micro-breaks, breaks of 10 minutes or less, across 22 samples and 2,335 people. Their answer is honest, and it is mixed.

LiveFeeling better is not the same as doing more3 min▶

Micro-breaks raised vigour (an effect size of d = .36) and lowered fatigue (d = .35). Both were statistically significant and both were small: on Cohen's conventional benchmarks, 0.2 counts as small and 0.5 as medium. So people felt a bit more energetic and a bit less tired. That is a real result and worth having.

Performance is the other story. Across all tasks the effect was d = .16 and not statistically significant, which means the pooled studies could not rule out no effect at all. Breaks did help performance on less cognitively demanding tasks, and longer breaks went with bigger performance gains. So the honest sentence is: a three-minute break is likely to make you feel a little better; it is not shown to make your analysis faster or sharper.

Moves 5 and 6. Wrist circles: make loose fists, circle your wrists eight times each way, then spread your fingers wide. Circles only as wide as feels easy. Stop if you get tingling or numbness in your fingers. March and look far: march on the spot at a comfortable pace, look at something at least 6 metres away, a window or the far end of the room, and blink fully. Stop if you feel faint or short of breath. Session 5 goes deeper on eyes and wrists.

Self-studyThis is not tai chi or yoga2 min▶

Six moves in three minutes is a break, not a practice. It borrows nothing from a tradition and claims nothing a tradition claims. If the routine makes you curious about slow, whole-body movement, the Tai Chi course teaches it properly, with its own forms and history. If you want to understand stretching, poses and what yoga does and does not do, the Yoga Theory course covers that.

Breathing techniques and the physiology of stress are a separate subject again; Stress Control owns them.

Real world

"I feel better, but did I get more done?" A common reaction after the first week of desk breaks: the afternoons feel less heavy, and the to-do list looks about the same. On Albulescu's numbers, that is exactly what you would expect. Judge the routine on how you feel and on your longest sit, the two things it can move, not on output it was never shown to raise.

Build-along

Run it, then make it yours

★ 22 min · everyone builds

First everyone runs the routine once as drawn, with the timer if you have it open. Then you tailor it: keep what works, swap what does not, and write your version down. Priyanka's notes are constructed; yours will differ, and that is the point.

Clear a space. You need about an arm's reach around your chair and enough room to step one foot back. Push the chair in a little if it is in the way. Work clothes and work shoes are fine.

★ Step 1 · the spaceArm's reach on both sides: clear One step back behind me: clear Something 6 metres away I can look at: the window

Run moves 1 and 2, seated. Neck turns, five each side, then eight shoulder rolls. Thirty seconds each. Timer tip: the session 4 page has a routine timer that calls each move every 30 seconds, so you do not have to watch a clock.

★ Step 2 · 0:00 to 1:000:00 neck turns 5 each side, only as far as easy 0:30 shoulder rolls 8 slow rolls, lift, back, down

Stand for moves 3 and 4. This is the moment that ends your sitting bout. Stand and reach, four each side, then the hip opener, 20 seconds each side.

★ Step 3 · 1:00 to 2:001:00 STAND UP 1:00 stand and reach 4 each side, gentle lean 1:30 hip opener 20 s each side, stop at the first stretch

Wrists and march, moves 5 and 6. Stay standing. Wrist circles and a finger spread, then march on the spot while you look at something far away and blink fully. At 3:00, sit back down.

★ Step 4 · 2:00 to 3:002:00 wrist circles 8 each way, then spread fingers 2:30 march, look far 6 m or more away, blink fully 3:00 sit back down

Apply the swap rule. If a move hurts or is impossible for you, replace it with an easier move for the same area, or drop it. The stop-if lines still apply to every swap. The one thing worth keeping is a moment of standing, because that is what ends the bout. Priyanka's (constructed) knee complains in the hip opener, so she swaps it.

★ Step 5 · one easier swap per areaneck -> smaller turns, halfway, or look side to side with your eyes shoulders -> seated shoulder shrugs: lift and drop, no roll side reach -> one arm at a time, no lean hips -> stand holding the desk, shift weight slowly foot to foot wrists -> slowly open and close your hands march -> marching seated: lift one knee, then the other

Write your version on a card. Six lines, one per 30 seconds, with your swaps in. Put it where you will see it: under the monitor, on the back of your phone case, in the notes app. Session 6 turns it into a habit with a cue.

★ Step 6 · your card0:00 _____________________ (seated) 0:30 _____________________ (seated) 1:00 STAND _______________ 1:30 _____________________ 2:00 _____________________ 2:30 _____________________ 3:00 sit down My stop-if reminder: if it hurts, swap it or drop it.

Your version beats the drawn one. A routine with two swaps that you do every afternoon does more for your longest sit than a perfect one you skip. Your card is yours: nothing on these pages records it.

Homework

Before session 4

15 min
  1. Run your card twice tomorrow. Once late morning, once mid-afternoon. Tick each move you did and note any you swapped or dropped.
  2. Check every move against its stop-if line. If anything produced dizziness, tingling, numbness or sharp pain, drop it and write down which move and what you felt. If a symptom carries on, use the NHS list in Part 1.
  3. Rate how you felt. Straight after each run, one word for energy and one for tiredness. Albulescu's review measured feelings; this is your own small version. Do not expect your output to change.
  4. Bring your day's schedule. Start, end, meetings and lunch for a normal working day. Session 4 drops your routine into it and shows which breaks your meetings swallow.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · During wrist circles you feel tingling in your fingers. What do you do?

Tingling or numbness in the fingers is the stop-if line for move 5. Stopping means stopping, not finishing the count. If symptoms persist, a professional should look, not a routine.

2 · What did Albulescu and colleagues' 2022 micro-break meta-analysis find for performance?

Vigour (d = .36) and fatigue (d = .35) moved significantly, both small. Overall performance did not reach significance. Longer breaks went with bigger performance boosts.

3 · Why does the routine make you stand up for moves 3 to 6?

The stand-up is what breaks the sit. Dunstan's lab trial used walking breaks, not this routine, so no disease claim follows; the routine's sure effect is ending the bout.
