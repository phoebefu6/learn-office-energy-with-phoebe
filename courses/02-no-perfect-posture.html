Part 1 · covers Slater et al. 2019 vs the NHS neck pain page

Sit up straight, says who?

5 min live

In 2019 a group of physiotherapists led by David Slater published a short viewpoint in the Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy with a blunt title: "Sit up straight": time to re-evaluate. On the same question, the NHS page on neck pain still lists bad posture as a common cause and tells you to sit upright. Both are sources a careful person would trust. Read them side by side before deciding anything.

LiveWhat the viewpoint argues3 min▶

Slater and colleagues make a narrow, careful claim. The belief that spinal pain is caused by sitting or bending "incorrectly" is widespread, among the public and among clinicians, but it lacks strong evidence. People with slumped postures do not reliably have more pain than people who sit tall, and people in pain do not share one obviously wrong shape. Meanwhile a posture industry has flourished anyway, selling the idea that there is one right way to hold your body.

Notice what they do not say. They do not say posture never matters to anyone, and they do not say sitting all day is fine. It is a viewpoint, an argued opinion piece built on the research, not a new trial. Its message for Priyanka is that the expensive chair and the slouch buzzer are aimed at a cause that has not been shown to be the cause.

Self-studyWhy the NHS still says sit upright, and how to hold both4 min▶

The NHS neck pain page, read on 2026-10-12, lists bad posture, for example sitting hunched over a desk, among the common causes and advises sitting upright. In the same breath it advises not keeping your neck in one position for a long time. Public health pages are written to be short, practical and safe for millions of readers, and "sit upright" is easy advice to follow that is unlikely to hurt anyone.

So the two sources differ in emphasis: one says a correct position has not been shown to exist, the other names a better and a worse one. Nobody has settled that, and these pages will not pretend to. What both say, explicitly, is that staying in one position for long is a problem. That overlap is solid ground, and it is where the rest of tonight builds: vary the position, rather than perfecting it.

A useful habit when two good sources disagree: write down what each one actually claims, find the sentence they share, and act on that sentence first. The disagreement is real information; it tells you the evidence is thinner than either source's tone suggests.

Real world

The slouch buzzer in the desk drawer. A familiar office pattern: someone buys a posture corrector or a wearable that buzzes when they slump, wears it diligently for a week or two, finds it holds them stiffly in one shape, and the gadget ends up in a drawer. Notice what it was rewarding: holding one position, which is the one thing both sources warn against. A reminder to change position would have been aimed at the shared rule; a reminder to hold a position is aimed at the contested one.

Part 2 · covers "the next posture is the best posture" and the Cochrane sit-stand evidence

The best posture is the next one

5 min live

Physiotherapists have a saying for the shared rule: the best posture is the next posture. If changing position is the point, the obvious tool is a desk that lets you stand. In 2018 a Cochrane review led by Nipun Shrestha pooled 34 workplace studies with 3,397 people, all in high-income countries, to ask how much sitting such interventions really remove.

LiveWhat a sit-stand desk does and does not do3 min▶

In the first three months, sit-stand desks cut sitting by about 100 minutes per workday (95% CI 116 to 84 minutes; 10 studies; low-quality evidence). By 3 to 12 months the two studies that kept measuring found about 57 minutes. The desk keeps working, but roughly half as hard: the novelty wears off and the desk spends more time in the down position. Past 12 months there is simply no evidence either way.

Two things follow for Priyanka. First, standing all day is not the goal; replacing one fixed position with another fixed position misses the shared rule from Part 1. The point is change. Second, the cheapest row on the chart is not a desk at all: short breaks of 1 to 2 minutes every half hour cut sitting by about 40 minutes a day compared with two 15-minute breaks, in a single low-quality study. Small, frequent and free is a serious option, not a consolation prize.

Self-studyThe phrase is a clinical saying, not a study3 min▶

"The best posture is the next posture" is a common clinical phrase among physiotherapists and ergonomists. No single source for it was found, and no trial tested the sentence itself. Treat it as a memorable summary of the overlap in Part 1, not as a finding. It is a good summary precisely because it stays inside what both sources say.

The review also lists what it could not find: no studies at all of standing or walking meetings, treadmill and cycling desks with unclear or inconsistent results, and nothing beyond a year. Computer prompts plus information showed about 14 minutes less sitting in the short term, which was not statistically significant, and about 55 minutes in the medium term in one study. Session 6 returns to prompts, because they target the habit rather than the furniture.

Real world

The standing desk that became a shelf. A common office pattern: a team gets sit-stand desks, the first weeks are full of people standing for calls, and a few months later most desks sit permanently at seated height with a plant on the control panel. The Cochrane numbers tell the same story more politely: 100 minutes becomes 57. If you have a sit-stand desk, the useful question is not whether you own one but how often it moves.

Part 3 · covers OSHA monitor basics

Three numbers for the screen

5 min live

The contested part is whether a single correct posture exists. The less contested part is the screen: where it sits decides which way your head has to go to read it. The US Occupational Safety and Health Administration's computer workstations guide gives three numbers that cover most desks.

LiveThe three numbers3 min▶

Distance: 50 to 100 cm, or 20 to 40 inches, from your eyes to the screen. A quick check is an outstretched arm: if your fingertips roughly reach the screen, you are in the range. Height: the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level. Angle: the centre of the screen 15 to 20 degrees below your horizontal eye line, so you look slightly down at what you read most.

These are comfort guides from an occupational safety agency, not results of a trial on pain. Their logic is plain: a screen far too low pulls your head forward and down, a screen too high tips it back, and a screen too close or too far makes you lean. A screen in the range lets you sit in several positions and still read it, which is what the shared rule needs.

Self-studyLaptops and two screens3 min▶

A laptop forces a trade: if the keyboard is in a good place, the screen is too low; if the screen is high enough, the keyboard is too high. The usual answer is to separate them. Raise the laptop on a stand or a stack of books until the top of the screen reaches the guide, and use a separate keyboard and mouse at desk level. For a day of laptop-only work away from your desk, change position more often instead.

With two screens, put the one you use most directly in front of you, at the three numbers, and the second one beside it. If you use both equally, centre the pair on the seam between them. The guidance reviewed for these pages gives no extra numbers for two-screen setups, so none are given here; the test is whether you can read your main screen without twisting.

Real world

The hot desk at the wrong height. In many offices with shared desks, the monitor arm stays wherever the last person left it, and most people sit down and work around it all day rather than spend a minute adjusting it. The three numbers turn a vague "this feels off" into a thirty-second check you can run every morning on whichever desk you get.

Build-along

Ten-minute desk setup

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Do this at the desk you will use tomorrow, or with the desk in front of you now. Each step takes a minute or two, none needs new equipment, and the last one matters most: a written list of the positions you will rotate through, because the shared rule is change, not a perfect setup. Treat every step as a comfort guide, not a cure.

Feet and chair. Adjust the chair height so your feet rest flat on the floor and your thighs are roughly level. If the chair has to go high to reach the desk and your feet dangle, put a box or a stack of paper under them. Priyanka (constructed) finds her chair was set for someone taller; one lever fixes it.

★ Step 1 · feet and chairFeet flat on the floor (or on a footrest)? yes / no Thighs roughly level, not sloping down? yes / no Changed: ______________________________

The arm's-length screen check. Sit back and stretch one arm straight toward the screen. Fingertips near the glass puts you roughly in OSHA's 50 to 100 cm range. Much closer, push the screen back; much further, bring it in or enlarge the text rather than leaning forward.

★ Step 2 · arm's lengthFingertips reach the screen? yes / closer / further Target: 50 to 100 cm (20 to 40 in), eyes to screen Changed: ______________________________

Top of the screen against your eye line. Look straight ahead with your head level. The top edge of the screen should be at or slightly below that line, which puts the centre of the screen 15 to 20 degrees below it. If the top edge is well below your eyes, raise the screen; if it is above them, lower it.

★ Step 3 · screen heightTop edge of screen vs eye line: above / level / slightly below / far below Goal: at or slightly below; centre 15-20 degrees below Changed: ______________________________

Keyboard and mouse close. Bring the keyboard near the desk edge so your elbows can rest near your sides, and keep the mouse right beside it rather than at arm's stretch. Session 5 returns to wrists in detail.

★ Step 4 · keyboard and mouseElbows near my sides while typing? yes / no Mouse next to the keyboard, not out at a reach? yes / no Changed: ______________________________

Laptop riser or books. If you work on a laptop, raise it until step 3 passes, using a stand or a stack of books, and plug in a separate keyboard and mouse so step 4 still passes. No separate keyboard today? Write it down as the one purchase that is actually worth making, ahead of any chair.

★ Step 5 · laptopLaptop raised so the top edge meets step 3? yes / no / not a laptop Separate keyboard and mouse? yes / no / need one Raised with: books / stand / box / ________

Write your "next posture" list. Three positions you will rotate through, with a cue for each switch. Change every 30 minutes or so, matching the short breaks in the Cochrane review. Hold each for as long as it stays comfortable, move only as far as feels easy, and stand for 1 to 2 minutes at least every half hour. Stop if a position brings on pain, numbness or tingling, and if that keeps happening, see a GP or physiotherapist.

★ Step 6 · your next posture listPosition A: sit back, supported by the chair Position B: sit forward, feet flat, forearms on the desk Position C: stand, 1-2 minutes or longer if you have a sit-stand desk Switch cue: every ~30 min & at the start of every call Stop if: pain, numbness or tingling -> change position, and see a GP if it keeps happening

When to stop adjusting and see someone. The NHS advises seeing a GP for neck pain that has not gone after a few weeks, when painkillers are not working, or if you have pins and needles or a cold arm. A desk setup will not fix any of those, and session 6 has the full list for back pain.

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What do Slater and colleagues (2019) argue in their JOSPT viewpoint?

It is a viewpoint, not a trial, and its claim is narrow: the "correct posture" belief is not well supported. The NHS page still names bad posture as a common cause; both agree that holding one position for long is the problem.

2 · What did the 2018 Cochrane review find about sit-stand desks over time?

The short-term figure comes from 10 studies of low-quality evidence; only 2 studies measured at 3 to 12 months, where the effect had roughly halved. Nothing was measured past a year, and the review did not test pain prevention.

3 · Where does OSHA's computer workstations guide put the monitor?

The three numbers: distance 50 to 100 cm (20 to 40 inches), top at or slightly below eye level, centre 15 to 20 degrees down. They are comfort guides from a safety agency, not a cure for pain.
